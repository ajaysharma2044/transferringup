The Common App rewrote its transfer essay prompt a few cycles back, and applicants keep handing in answers to the old one. The current wording, the one on your screen for the spring and fall 2027 cycles, asks you to "provide a statement discussing your educational path," then closes with the question that carries the grade: "How does continuing your education at a new institution help you achieve your future goals?" You get 250 to 650 words. Read it twice, because it never asks what went wrong at your current school, and it never asks you to prove you are unhappy. It asks for a path and a destination.

That shift tells you how the reading works now. The committee holds your transcript in one hand and your statement in the other, and the statement's job is to give the transcript a direction. Cornell prints the same 250-to-650-word question on its own transfer applicant page, so the wording follows you from the main application into the school-specific stack.

## What the reader is measuring

A transfer file gets judged on a blunt standard: what you did with the seat you already have. The committee is betting that you will arrive, do the work, and finish a degree in roughly two years, and every sentence you write either supports that bet or wastes space. The schools say versions of this out loud. Stanford's transfer instructions ask for your reasons for transferring and the objectives you hope to achieve in one 250-to-650-word statement, then add short questions capped at 50 words each, a format that punishes padding on contact. UVA tells second-year applicants that high school and college records get read together, and third-year applicants that college work dominates, which means the essay of a college sophomore is standing on top of whatever the transcript already says. UNC's admissions office describes its essays as the place readers hear your voice and meet the person behind the numbers. Put those together and the assignment is concrete evidence of direction, delivered in your own voice, sized for small boxes.

## The prompts named schools add on top

| School | What transfer applicants answer | Limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Columbia | "Please explain why you are interested in transferring from your current institution." | 400 to 600 words |
| Cornell | A supplement specific to the undergraduate college. CALS, for example, asks why your major and why you want to pursue it at CALS | 3,000 characters |
| Michigan | Three essays: your reasons for transferring and your objectives, a community you belong to and your place in it, and why your chosen U-M school's curriculum | 1,500, 1,500, and 2,750 characters |
| Stanford | Reasons for transferring and objectives, plus a set of short answers | 250 to 650 words, short answers at 50 |

All four come from each school's admissions and application materials as of August 2026. Two details in that table are worth stealing. Columbia says outright that this essay does not have to be specific to Columbia, the committee wants your leaving logic to stand on its own, which is why a Columbia essay built entirely from flattery dies early. And Michigan splits the work into three separate containers, so an answer that smears why-transfer reasoning into the why-Michigan box wastes the only space either question gets. Michigan's community prompt is also broader than people assume. The official wording lets you define community through "shared geography, religion, ethnicity, income, cuisine, interest, race, ideology, or intellectual heritage," which means your night-shift kitchen crew or your college's chess club qualifies as fully as a hometown does. If one of the first two is your target, I wrote school-level breakdowns: [how to transfer to Cornell](/blog/how-to-transfer-to-cornell) and [how to transfer to Columbia](/blog/how-to-transfer-to-columbia).

## Transfer essay examples: weak lines and rewrites

Every example line below is one I wrote for this article to show a shape. No admitted student's essay appears on this page, and anyone who shows you "the essay that got someone into Stanford" is selling something.

### The opening

Weak: "I have realized my current school no longer challenges me, and I am ready for an environment that will push me to grow."

Strong: "After my second semester of organic chemistry I wanted enzyme kinetics, and my college's biochemistry track stops at the intro level, with no faculty running a wet lab."

The second line names the wall by its course title. A reader can verify it, which means a reader can believe it, and it also proves you know your current school's catalog well enough to have hit its edge.

### The setback

Weak: "My first-year GPA does not reflect my true abilities."

Strong: "Drifting through three intro courses taught me I was avoiding the quantitative work I wanted, so this year I took the two hardest statistics courses my college offers and earned an A in both."

The weak line asks the reader to discount evidence. The strong line supplies newer evidence. My own file worked this way: the application that got me into Cornell gave one clause to the 2.9 I carried out of high school and every remaining sentence to the eighteen months after it. If your number is the thing keeping you up at night, I wrote a whole piece on [transferring with a low GPA](/blog/can-you-transfer-with-a-low-gpa), including how to use the additional information section for the factual version of what happened.

### The why-us

Weak: "I admire the world-class faculty and the vibrant, diverse community."

Strong: "I want to join Professor Lim's soft robotics lab because my Arduino prosthetics project hit the ceiling of what I could build alone."

You might worry that this much specificity reads as pandering. It reads as homework, and committees reward homework. Run the swap test on whatever you draft: if another school's name fits your supplement without breaking a single sentence, start over.

## A structure that holds for 650 words

Open inside one specific academic moment at your current school, a class, a project, a lab hour, with no throat clearing about a lifelong love of learning. Name the limit you hit there in factual terms, the course sequence that ends, the research with no home. Then show what you built anyway, because the semesters you spent stuck are the ones the committee scores hardest. Give the target school two or three named continuations of that work, the sequence, the professor, the program, each with a sentence of reasoning attached. Close on what the two years there produce, stated like a plan. Braid the why-leave and the why-here into one argument the whole way through, since a reader should never feel two essays stapled together. Social fit, location, and cost can appear as honest secondary reasons, and they should never be the headline.

On the word budget, my working split for a 650-word statement is 150 words inside the opening moment, 150 on the limit you hit, 200 on what you built at the school you have, and 150 on the target and the plan. Drafts that die usually spent 400 of their 650 words on dissatisfaction, which leaves the reader holding a complaint with a school name attached.

Look, the version of this essay that works takes weeks. The calendar is tighter than it feels in August: spring 2027 deadlines sit in October and November 2026, and the fall 2027 wave arrives in late winter, with Cornell's application closing March 15, 2027. Writing the first draft the same week the application is due produces the flattery essay every time.

## Mistakes that sink transfer essays

The classic one is badmouthing your current school. The committee assumes whatever tone you take about your old school is the tone you will someday take about theirs, so frame the move as growth toward something. Recycling one why-us across five supplements comes next, and readers catch it fast, because a recycled paragraph names nothing the school would recognize as its own. Restating your activities list burns space the essay was given for reasoning, so if a sentence could sit as a bullet in the activities section, cut it. And leaving an obvious GPA dip unexplained anywhere in the file invites the committee to write the explanation themselves, which will be less generous than yours. The additional information section exists for those three factual sentences. Use it.
